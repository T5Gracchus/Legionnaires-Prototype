## The core premise

During the destruction of Varus’s army in **9 CE**, a Roman century is cut off while recovering the wounded. Its centurion brings the survivors through the ambush, only to discover that their apparent deliverance has placed them in the hands of captors from beyond their world.

The baggage rescue is the opening tutorial. Capture ends that mission; the heroes are subsequently sold to an alien in disguise and forced to fight wars beyond their world. Their existing military skills and relationships must remain meaningful through the transition.

The tactical game is central, with lighter story and strategy layers around it. The wider campaign envisions a larger roster and deployments of approximately five to twelve–fifteen soldiers; neither the six-person Roman test nor the four-person off-world laboratory fixes the permanent deployment size.

## The Roman trio

- **The centurion** is a veteran from Rome, both a formidable fighter and a mentor/commander. He is a childhood friend of the brothers’ craftsman father and has undertaken to watch over the sons.
- **The brothers** are Rome-born, talented, and relatively untested. One favors javelins and defensive fighting; the other favors speed and sword offense. Their equipment is broadly similar—their different skills and temperaments carry their identities.
- Supporting legionnaires belong to the wider century. They are not substitutes for the three leads, nor is a carry-only wounded mission casualty the same thing as a revivable playable hero.

Names, exact ages, the father’s specific craft, legion membership, detailed capture/sale staging, and reveal timing remain open. These gaps do not authorize inventing settled biography. D37 reaffirms the trio, Rome origins, rescue opening, deployment range, and three separate action categories. D38 recognizes the existing tutorial without permanently adopting its numerical values.

## Galactic setting and authorities

The godlike enforcers and the galactic bureaucracy are **the same society** (D32). The Confederation is the accepted institutional elaboration: Tier One powers acting through a common institution (D36). Preventing catastrophic warfare and the earlier concern about a universe-ending danger can coexist. Exact threats, membership, government, powers, and numerical civilization scales are undefined.

The restriction on advanced civilizations contacting/interfering with less advanced civilizations remains established. Precise legality around the Romans’ circumstances is unresolved: D33/D35 reopen illegal abduction, species-dependent permission for slavery, and a proxy-war loophole as possibilities. Do not present those possibilities as settled laws or as an approved explanation for every game rule.

Initial captors, the disguised buyer, the squad’s employer, and the ship’s command need not be the same actor. Keep these roles distinct until identities and sponsorship are chosen. An ambitious Tier Two captor civilization constrained by a Great Armistice is favored proposal P11, not a fully settled institutional history.

## Advanced technology and the alien veteran

An alien veteran joins the Romans early to teach the consequences of advanced technology (D34). He is separate from the centurion and is not an added member of the Roman rescue detachment.

His exact mission, identity, species, augmentation, and fate are undecided. Scripted death, inevitable disintegration, and permanent loss are **not approved requirements**. Test the power-versus-exposure rule clearly before authoring a fixed lesson around him.

The prototype now experiments with stronger equipped gear shortening the safe mission window and bringing enforcement. That is an authorized playable experiment; its exact signature formula, deadlines, target-selection rule, and two-turn grace window are provisional mechanics, not a complete statement of campaign law. An active-use signature meter followed by a separate response timer remains an alternative concept rather than the current implementation.

## What the current missions mean for the story

There are ten playable missions: the Roman opening and nine independent off-world trials. The Roman opening communicates the trio’s relationship, wounded rescue, and capture ending. Its debrief reports who was evacuated and who remains standing without inventing off-screen deaths.

The off-world missions test combinations of species, equipment, technology pressure, terrain, enemy support, and objectives: recovery, relay sabotage, captive rescue, summit control, wave defense, demolition, escort, linked controls, and an ordered archive raid.

Their level names, alien names, numerical balance, loss rules, and mission details are **reversible laboratory choices**. They are not a chronological ten-mission campaign, proof of a particular legal loophole, or a fixed alien-veteran storyline. Free bench recruitment is separate from freeing map captives. A lost or vaporized trial recruit does not establish a permanent campaign death rule.

## Questions to resolve through play and design

1. Does the Roman trio remain recognizable after the setting and equipment change?
2. Do players understand power, signature, detection, enforcement, and extraction as distinct ideas?
3. Do species abilities create useful tactical combinations without making advanced weapons the only enjoyable option?
4. What can the centurion teach in play, and what should the alien veteran teach that he cannot?
5. How should injury, revival, capture, augmentation, regeneration, and return between missions relate?
6. What freedom, loyalty, return-home, or other long-term ending is intended? Do not infer an approved ending.
7. How little ship/strategy interface can support meaningful preparation and relationships while keeping tactics dominant?

The current laboratory can supply evidence about readability, pace, equipment choices, abandoned objectives, and extraction. It cannot settle the story’s legal or moral framework through its implementation details alone.
